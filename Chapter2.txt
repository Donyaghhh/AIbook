The rain started the day Mira buried her father, and it did not stop.

She drove north until the roads ran out, then walked.
The forest took her in without comment—pines dark as wet slate, ferns uncurling under the downpour, a floor of moss that swallowed her footsteps whole.
She had no plan. She only knew she couldn't stay in that house with its ticking clocks and its empty chair.

By dusk she found a cabin. No smoke, no lights, just a door slightly ajar.

Inside, a fire burned in the grate, though no one had fed it. A kettle sang on the stove. And on the table sat two cups, both already warm.

Mira sat down, because her legs had nothing left to give. She drank the tea.
It tasted like the way her father used to hum while fixing things—that low, tuneless sound that meant everything was going to be fine.

She slept.

In the morning, the rain had slowed to a drizzle, and on the table there was only one cup. Hers. The other was washed and put away.

Mira understood, in the way you understand things in dreams, that she had been given exactly one night.
Not to say goodbye—she'd had years for that. Just to be somewhere warm while the worst of it passed through her, like weather.

She stepped outside. The forest was dripping and green and alive.

She started walking back.
